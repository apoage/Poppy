**Executive Summary**
The "dual collapse" phenomenon you describe—where a policy converges to a single action (HOLD) while an auxiliary state-prediction head learns to output a trivial "no change"—is a known, interconnected failure mode in model-based RL. It arises from a vicious cycle: a collapsing policy reduces the data distribution's diversity, making a constant predictor optimal, and that collapsed predictor then fails to provide useful gradients for policy improvement. This issue is documented in recent literature under terms like **policy entropy collapse**, **model collapse**, **representation collapse**, and **auxiliary-task trivialization**.

## ❓ Answers to Your Specific Questions

### 1. How do MuZero/AlphaZero implementations prevent policy collapse during training?
- **Entropy loss term**: Standard MuZero includes a policy entropy term in the loss, but its weight must be carefully tuned. Many implementations (e.g., MuZero‑General) use a fixed entropy coefficient, which may be insufficient.
- **MCTS exploration**: The UCB formula in MCTS injects exploration via the **exploration constant** (c_puct). Higher values encourage more uniform search, but this alone does not prevent neural‑network policy collapse.
- **Adaptive entropy scheduling**: Some practitioners schedule the entropy coefficient inversely with training steps or use **population‑based training** to adapt it dynamically.
- **Architectural tricks**: **RobustZero** uses an asymmetric projection‑predictor network with stop‑gradient to avoid representation collapse, which indirectly supports policy diversity[reference:20].

### 2. What causes auxiliary prediction heads to learn trivial solutions in model‑based RL?
- **Shortcut learning**: If the loss can be minimized by outputting a constant (e.g., “no change”), the network will exploit that shortcut[reference:21].
- **Distribution shift**: As the policy collapses, the training data for the auxiliary head becomes monotonous (e.g., only flat trajectories), making the trivial solution optimal.
- **Symmetry in the architecture**: Siamese‑style networks without asymmetry can collapse because both branches can jointly move toward a constant output.

### 3. How should entropy regularization be weighted relative to policy loss?
- **Dynamic weighting**: The paper “The Entropy Mechanism…” shows that entropy dynamics are driven by covariance between action probability and advantage; they propose **Clip‑Cov** and **KL‑Cov** to adaptively constrain updates[reference:22].
- **Rule of thumb**: Many MuZero implementations start with an entropy coefficient of ~0.01‑0.05 and decay it linearly or with a cosine schedule. However, **adaptive methods** that track entropy directly (e.g., target entropy scheduling) are more robust.
- **Regularization vs. reward**: The entropy term should be large enough to counteract the policy’s tendency to become deterministic early, but not so large that it overwhelms the value/reward signal.

### 4. Are there techniques to decouple policy and world model training to prevent coupled collapse?
- **Separate data buffers**: Train the world model on a **fixed replay buffer** that includes diverse historical data, rather than solely on policy‑generated trajectories.
- **Freezing components**: Periodically freeze the policy network and train the world model alone, or vice‑versa.
- **Auxiliary task diversification**: Add multiple auxiliary tasks (e.g., reward prediction, inverse dynamics, time‑contrastive learning) to force the representation to retain information beyond the trivial solution.
- **Intrinsic motivation**: Use **curiosity bonuses** (prediction error) or **information‑gain bonuses** to encourage exploration of novel states, which diversifies the data for the world model.

### 5. What is the relationship between MCTS simulation count and policy entropy?
- **Higher simulation counts** typically lead to **more refined policy targets** (search policy), which can lower entropy because the search concentrates on the best actions.
- However, **more simulations also improve value estimates**, which can reduce premature convergence by providing better credit assignment.
- In practice, **too few simulations** may leave the policy target noisy, causing the neural network to overfit to suboptimal actions; **too many simulations** can produce overly sharp policy targets that drive entropy collapse. A common remedy is to **scale the entropy coefficient inversely with simulation count**.

### 6. How do exploration bonuses (UCB, intrinsic curiosity) interact with learned value functions?
- **UCB (Upper Confidence Bound)** in MCTS adds an exploration bonus that is **independent of the learned value function**. This helps explore actions that the value network might underestimate.
- **Intrinsic curiosity modules** (e.g., prediction‑error bonuses) provide a **separate reward signal** that can conflict with the extrinsic reward. If not carefully balanced, they can lead to **distraction** or **catastrophic forgetting**.
- **Integrated approaches** like **Free‑Energy Minimization** blend expected reward with information gain, aiming to balance exploitation and exploration without degrading the value function[reference:23].

### 7. What are known failure modes when training world models on policy‑generated data?
- **Distribution shift**: The world model is trained on data generated by the current policy, which may become increasingly narrow, leading to **model bias** and **overfitting** to the policy’s limited experience.
- **Compounding error**: Small errors in the world model can compound over long rollouts, causing the policy to exploit **model hallucinations**.
- **Collapse to trivial dynamics**: As observed in your case, the world model may learn to predict “no change” because the policy’s actions produce little variation in the observed states.
- **Loss of plasticity**: The world model may **stop learning** new dynamics once the policy collapses, because the gradient signals become vanishingly small.

## 💡 Recommended Solutions & Practical Steps

Based on the literature, here are concrete steps to mitigate coupled policy‑prediction collapse:

| Solution | How It Addresses the Problem | Reference |
|----------|-------------------------------|-----------|
| **Asymmetric auxiliary architecture** | Add a predictor network on one branch and apply stop‑gradient to the other branch to prevent trivial constant solutions. | RobustZero[reference:24] |
| **Entropy‑aware update clipping** | Use Clip‑Cov or KL‑Cov to restrict updates for actions with high covariance between probability and advantage, slowing entropy collapse. | “The Entropy Mechanism…”[reference:25] |
| **Adam hyperparameter tuning** | Set β₁ = β₂ (e.g., both 0.9) and use aggressive L2 regularization to prevent sudden weight changes that cause collapse. | “Overcoming Policy Collapse…”[reference:26] |
| **Auxiliary task diversification** | Include multiple auxiliary prediction tasks (e.g., reward prediction, inverse dynamics, longer‑horizon state prediction) to force the representation to retain relevant information. | “Predictive auxiliary objectives…”[reference:27] |
| **Intrinsic exploration bonuses** | Incorporate information‑gain or prediction‑error bonuses to encourage exploration of novel states, diversifying the data for the world model. | “Boosting MCTS with Free Energy Minimization”[reference:28] |
| **Separate data buffers** | Train the world model on a fixed replay buffer that contains diverse historical data, not just recent policy trajectories. | Common practice in model‑based RL (e.g., Dreamer) |
| **Periodic resets / population‑based training** | Occasionally reset the policy network or maintain a population of policies to reintroduce diversity. | Used in AlphaStar and OpenAI Five |
| **Entropy scheduling** | Dynamically adjust the entropy coefficient based on current entropy (e.g., target entropy scheduling) or training progress. | Common in PPO implementations |

## 🎯 Conclusion

The coupled policy‑prediction collapse you observe is a documented challenge in model‑based RL. It stems from a **positive feedback loop** between a narrowing policy and a world model that adapts to that narrow data distribution. Breaking this loop requires **architectural interventions** (e.g., asymmetric networks, stop‑gradient), **training‑time strategies** (e.g., entropy‑aware updates, auxiliary task diversification), and **exploration bonuses** to maintain diversity. The literature suggests that no single fix is sufficient; a combination of the techniques above is likely needed to stabilize training.

**Recommended immediate actions**:
1.  Implement an **asymmetric predictor‑stop‑gradient** structure for your auxiliary prediction head.
2.  **Dynamically schedule the entropy coefficient** (e.g., using Clip‑Cov or a target entropy schedule).
3.  **Add an intrinsic exploration bonus** (e.g., prediction error) to encourage the policy to generate non‑flat trajectories.
4.  **Regularize the optimizer** (adjust Adam β₁/β₂, increase L2 weight decay) to prevent sudden collapse.

By adopting these measures, you can decouple the policy and prediction collapses and move toward a more robust training regime.
