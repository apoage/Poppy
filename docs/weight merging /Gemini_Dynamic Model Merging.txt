The objective to merge two training branches, characterized by distinct data distributions but identical MuZero-style architectures, into a single agent with high oracle agreement requires navigating the complex geometry of high-dimensional loss landscapes. This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the theoretical mechanisms, algorithmic strategies, and practical implementations required to achieve this integration.We posit that standard Federated Averaging (FedAvg) is insufficient for this task due to the high probability of "symmetry breaking" and the formation of high-loss barriers between the two branches, despite their shared initialization. The disparate nature of stochastic diffusion data (Branch A) and deterministic rule-based patterns (Branch B) drives the optimization trajectories into topologically distinct basins of attraction. To resolve this, advanced merging strategies must be employed that account for parameter permutation invariance (Git Re-Basin), gradient conflict resolution (TIES-Merging), and curvature-aware importance weighting (Fisher Merging).This document details eight specific weight merging strategies, five compatibility metrics, and a robust progressive alignment schedule. We provide mathematical formulations and JAX/Flax implementation sketches for each, alongside a critical analysis of their computational costs and failure modes. Furthermore, we address the critical issue of catastrophic forgetting through Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) and Experience Replay, proposing a dynamic, divergence-triggered merging framework that adapts to the non-stationary nature of financial trading environments.1. Theoretical Foundations of Model MergingTo engineer a successful merging strategy for a trading agent trained on disjoint data sources, one must first deconstruct the underlying geometric and probabilistic assumptions that govern neural network optimization. The success or failure of combining weights from Branch A (Bella) and Branch B (Synthetic) is determined not merely by their individual performance, but by the topological properties of the path connecting their respective solutions in parameter space.1.1 The Geometry of Loss Landscapes and Mode ConnectivityThe central theoretical construct in model merging is the concept of Mode Connectivity. In the non-convex loss landscapes typical of deep neural networks, there exist infinitely many local minima that achieve comparable performance. A "mode" refers to one such basin of attraction.Linear Mode Connectivity (LMC)The simplest merging strategy, averaging weights, relies on the strong assumption of Linear Mode Connectivity (LMC). In your specific context, Branch A (trained on diffusion data) and Branch B (trained on rules) are pushed by gradients derived from fundamentally different manifolds. While they share an initialization $\theta_0$, the gradients $\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{L}_{diff}$ and $\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{L}_{rule}$ are likely orthogonal or even conflicting. Over time, as training progresses, the trajectory of $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$ diverges. If they enter distinct basins separated by a high-loss region (a "barrier"), LMC breaks. Averaging two models separated by a barrier results in a merged model $\theta_{avg}$ that sits atop a "ridge" of high loss—a phenomenon often observed as a sudden collapse in agent performance post-merge.The Basin Structure in Federated SettingsRecent research into the "Loss Basin" theory suggests that models trained with the same initialization often remain in the same "macro-basin" for a significant portion of training. However, the definition of a basin is nuanced. While they may be connected by non-linear paths of low loss, FedAvg forces a traversal along a linear chord. If the basin is curved (non-convex), the chord cuts through the wall of the basin, leading to high loss.The severity of this curvature is dictated by the Hessian of the loss function. Financial data, being inherently noisy and non-stationary, often results in "sharp" minima where the loss rises steeply in all directions. Rule-based data, being cleaner and deterministic, often results in "flatter" minima. Merging a sharp model with a flat model requires careful handling of weight magnitudes to prevent the sharp (unstable) solution from dominating the flat (robust) one.1.2 Neural Network Symmetries and Permutation InvarianceA 15-million parameter Transformer model exhibits massive redundancy due to Permutation Symmetry. For any given hidden layer with $H$ neurons, there are $H!$ possible permutations of those neurons that result in an identical functional output, provided the weights of the incoming and outgoing layers are permuted accordingly.The "Symmetry Breaking" PhenomenonEven with identical initialization, stochastic gradient descent (SGD) introduces noise. In Branch A, the network might learn to encode "market volatility" in neuron #42 of the encoder. In Branch B, the same concept might be encoded in neuron #105.Functional Mismatch: If we naively average these weights (FedAvg), we align neuron #42 from A with neuron #42 from B. Since B's neuron #42 might be encoding "liquidity" (or nothing at all), the average destroys the feature selectivity of both.Feature Interference: This is a primary cause of merge failure in federated learning with non-IID data. The weights are not "aligned" semantically, even if they share the same architecture.Resolution: This necessitates techniques like Git Re-Basin , which explicitly solves for the permutation matrix $P$ that aligns the hidden units of Branch B to Branch A before merging.1.3 The Lottery Ticket Hypothesis and Subnetwork SpecializationThe Lottery Ticket Hypothesis  posits that dense networks contain sparse subnetworks ("winning tickets") that are responsible for the model's performance. In your dual-branch setup, it is highly probable that Branch A finds a winning ticket utilizing a specific subset of the 15M parameters suitable for diffusion robustness, while Branch B activates a different, perhaps disjoint, subnetwork for mathematical pattern recognition.Constructive vs. Destructive Interference: If the winning tickets are disjoint (using different weights), simple averaging effectively dilutes the active weights of one branch with the inactive (near-zero or random) weights of the other, effectively halving the signal strength.Sign Conflict: Worse, if both branches use the same weight parameter for different purposes, they may update it in opposite directions (one positive, one negative). Averaging these results in a value near zero, effectively "killing" the neuron. This is the motivation behind TIES-Merging , which explicitly handles sign disagreements to prevent this destructive interference.2. def fed_avg(params_a, params_b, weight=0.5):
    """
    Computes the weighted average of two PyTrees (model checkpoints).
    
    Args:
        params_a: PyTree of parameters from Branch A.
        params_b: PyTree of parameters from Branch B.
        weight: Float, weight assigned to Branch A (default 0.5).
        
    Returns:
        Merged PyTree.
    """
    # jax.tree_util.tree_map allows element-wise operations 
    # over arbitrarily nested dictionaries/lists of arrays.
    return jax.tree_util.tree_map(
        lambda a, b: weight * a + (1.0 - weight) * b,
        params_a,
        params_b
    )

The update step remains:\JAX ImplementationThis is implemented as a wrapper around the loss function passed to optax.Pythonimport optax

def fedprox_loss_wrapper(task_loss_fn, mu=0.01):
    """
    Wraps a standard loss function with the FedProx regularization term.
    """
    def prox_loss(params, global_params, batch, **kwargs):
        # 1. Compute original task loss
        base_loss = task_loss_fn(params, batch, **kwargs)
        
        # 2. Compute L2 Euclidean distance between current and global params
        # tree_map computes (p-g)^2 for every tensor
        squared_diffs = jax.tree_util.tree_map(
            lambda p, g: jnp.sum(jnp.square(p - g)),
            params, 
            global_params
        )
        
        # 3. Sum all squared differences to get scalar L2 norm
        prox_term = sum(jax.tree_util.tree_leaves(squared_diffs))
        
        return base_loss + (mu / 2.0) * prox_term
        
    return prox_loss

It assumes the posterior distribution over the weights is Gaussian, with the covariance approximated by the inverse Fisher Information Matrix (FIM). Intuitively, the FIM measures how "sharp" the loss is with respect to each parameter.High Fisher Information: The loss is very sensitive to this parameter; changing it increases loss significantly. The parameter is "important."Low Fisher Information: The loss is flat; the parameter can vary without hurting performance.Fisher merging calculates a weighted average where each parameter is weighted by its own Fisher information (importance).FormulaUsing the Diagonal Approximation (ignoring correlations between parameters) reduces this to an element-wise operation:where $F_k^{(i)}$ is the $i$-th element of the diagonal Fisher matrix for model $k$.Computational CostMemory: Requires storing a diagonal FIM for each branch. For a 15M parameter model, this is an additional $15 \times 10^6 \times 4$ bytes $\approx 60$ MB per branch. This is negligible on modern GPUs.FLOPs: High. Computing the Fisher requires gradients. Specifically, the Empirical Fisher requires computing per-sample gradients (or expected gradients) over a validation batch.JAX ImplementationEfficient computation of the diagonal Fisher using jax.vmap is critical.Pythondef compute_diagonal_fisher(loss_fn, params, data_batch):
    """
    Computes diagonal Empirical Fisher Information Matrix (EFIM).
    EFIM ~ sum of squared gradients over the batch.
    """
    
    # 1. Define function to get gradient for a SINGLE sample
    # Note: For Fisher, we typically use the gradient of the log-likelihood 
    # of the *model's own prediction* (sampled y), not ground truth.
    def per_sample_grad(p, x, y_pred):
        return jax.grad(lambda pp, xx, yy: loss_fn(pp, xx, yy))(p, x, y_pred)

    # 2. Get predictions (to treat as targets for empirical fisher)
    # This assumes loss_fn handles the logic, or we pass fixed targets.
    # Here, simplified to standard gradient magnitude.
    
    grad_fn_vmapped = jax.vmap(per_sample_grad, in_axes=(None, 0, 0))
    
    # Compute gradients for the whole batch:
    # We need to construct dummy targets or use the model's own preds.
    # Assuming 'data_batch' contains (inputs, targets):
    inputs, targets = data_batch
    batch_grads = grad_fn_vmapped(params, inputs, targets)
    
    # 3. Square and mean over batch to get Diagonal Fisher
    fisher_diag = jax.tree_util.tree_map(
        lambda g: jnp.mean(jnp.square(g), axis=0),
        batch_grads
    )
    return fisher_diag

def fisher_merge(params_a, params_b, fisher_a, fisher_b, epsilon=1e-6):
    """
    Merges parameters weighted by their diagonal Fisher information.
    """
    return jax.tree_util.tree_map(
        lambda p_a, p_b, f_a, f_b: (f_a * p_a + f_b * p_b) / (f_a + f_b + epsilon),
        params_a, params_b, fisher_a, fisher_b
    )

These small changes accumulate and act as noise in the merged model.Sign Disagreement: One model increases a weight ($+0.1$) while another decreases it ($-0.1$). Averaging yields $0.0$, canceling out the feature entirely.TIES proposes a three-step process:Trim: Reset the bottom $k$% of updates (smallest magnitude) to zero.Elect Sign: Determine the "dominant" direction of change for each parameter across all models (by summing the updates).Disjoint Merge: Only keep updates that agree with the elected sign. Discard (zero out) the updates from branches that disagree.FormulaLet $\Delta_k = \theta_k - \theta_{init}$.Trim: $\hat{\Delta}_k = \Delta_k \odot \mathbb{I}(|\Delta_k| \in \text{top-}k\%)$Elect: $\sigma_{total} = \text{sgn}(\sum_k \hat{\Delta}_k)$Merge: $\theta_{new} = \theta_{init} + \frac{1}{|S|} \sum_{k \in S} \hat{\Delta}_k$, where $S = \{k \mid \text{sgn}(\hat{\Delta}_k) = \sigma_{total}\}$JAX ImplementationTIES requires access to the initialization checkpoint $\theta_{init}$.Pythondef ties_merging(params_a, params_b, params_init, k=0.2, merge_func='mean'):
    """
    TIES-Merging: Trims noise, resolves sign conflicts, and merges.
    """
    # 1. Calculate Deltas (Task Vectors)
    delta_a = jax.tree_util.tree_map(lambda a, i: a - i, params_a, params_init)
    delta_b = jax.tree_util.tree_map(lambda b, i: b - i, params_b, params_init)
    
    def process_layer(da, db):
        # We process each tensor independently
        
        # TRIM: Create masks for top-k magnitude values
        # Note: percentiles in JAX can be slow on large tensors; use approximations if needed.
        def get_trim_mask(d):
            threshold = jnp.percentile(jnp.abs(d), (1 - k) * 100)
            return jnp.abs(d) >= threshold
            
        mask_a = get_trim_mask(da)
        mask_b = get_trim_mask(db)
        
        # Zero out trimmed values (Redundancy Elimination)
        da_trim = da * mask_a
        db_trim = db * mask_b
        
        # ELECT SIGN: Sum of trimmed deltas defines the "consensus" direction
        total_delta = da_trim + db_trim
        elected_sign = jnp.sign(total_delta)
        
        # DISJOINT MERGE: Only keep updates that match the elected sign
        # Check sign agreement (including handling zeros correctly)
        valid_a = (jnp.sign(da_trim) == elected_sign) & (mask_a)
        valid_b = (jnp.sign(db_trim) == elected_sign) & (mask_b)
        
        # Calculate the merge
        # If both agree, average them. If only one agrees, take that one. 
        # If neither agrees (or both were trimmed), result is 0.
        count = valid_a.astype(jnp.float32) + valid_b.astype(jnp.float32)
        sum_updates = (da_trim * valid_a) + (db_trim * valid_b)
        
        # Avoid division by zero where count is 0
        final_delta = jnp.where(count > 0, sum_updates / count, 0.0)
        
        return final_delta

    merged_deltas = jax.tree_util.tree_map(process_layer, delta_a, delta_b)
    
    # Add merged deltas back to initialization
    return jax.tree_util.tree_map(lambda i, d: i + d, params_init, merged_deltas)

The "Greedy Soup" approach sequentially adds models to the average only if they improve performance on a held-out validation set.FormulaLet $M = \{ \theta_1, \theta_2, \dots, \theta_N \}$ be a set of checkpoints (e.g., from different epochs of Branch A and B).Sort models by validation accuracy.Initialize $\theta_{soup} = \theta_{best}$.For each candidate $\theta_c \in M$:$\theta_{candidate} = \text{Avg}(\theta_{soup}, \theta_c)$If $\text{Acc}(\theta_{candidate}) > \text{Acc}(\theta_{soup})$, then $\theta_{soup} \leftarrow \theta_{candidate}$.JAX Implementation SketchNote: This is an inference-time or validation-time loop, not a training operator.Pythondef greedy_model_soup(checkpoints, eval_fn):
    """
    Greedy Model Soup algorithm.
    checkpoints: List of PyTrees.
    eval_fn: Function taking params -> scalar score.
    """
    # 1. Sort by individual performance
    ranked_models = sorted(checkpoints, key=eval_fn, reverse=True)
    
    current_soup = ranked_models
    best_score = eval_fn(current_soup)
    ingredients = [current_soup]
    
    for i in range(1, len(ranked_models)):
        candidate_model = ranked_models[i]
        
        # Create temporary average of current soup + candidate
        # Note: In true soup, we average ALL ingredients uniformly.
        # New Soup = Mean(ingredients + [candidate])
        new_ingredients = ingredients + [candidate_model]
        
        # Efficient computation: new_soup = (current_soup * N + candidate) / (N+1)
        n = len(ingredients)
        new_soup = jax.tree_util.tree_map(
            lambda s, c: (s * n + c) / (n + 1),
            current_soup, candidate_model
        )
        
        score = eval_fn(new_soup)
        
        if score >= best_score:
            best_score = score
            current_soup = new_soup
            ingredients.append(candidate_model)
            
    return current_soup

def match_weights(params_a, params_b):
    """
    Simplified weight matching for a single dense layer.
    """
    # Extract weight matrices W_a, W_b: [Out, In]
    # We want to permute the rows (Out dimension) of B to match A
    
    # Cost matrix: Euclidean distance or correlation
    # Maximize dot product = Minimize angle
    # C[i, j] = - dot(row_a[i], row_b[j])
    w_a = params_a['kernel'] # Assuming [In, Out] standard Flax layout
    w_b = params_b['kernel']
    
    # We permute the 'Out' dimension (axis 1)
    # To use linear_sum_assignment, we need cost matrix size [Out, Out]
    # We compare the columns (neurons)
    cost_matrix = -np.dot(w_a.T, w_b) 
    
    row_ind, col_ind = linear_sum_assignment(cost_matrix)
    
    # col_ind is the permutation vector. 
    # The neuron at index col_ind[i] in B should move to index i.
    return col_ind

    def merge_leaf(path, a, b):
        # path is a tuple of dict keys, e.g., ('params', 'Encoder_0', 'Dense', 'kernel')
        path_str = "/".join([str(p) for p in path])

        else:

Evaluates the loss at the midpoint.\Implementation: Requires an extra forward pass on a validation batch.Threshold: Barrier should be near 0. If Barrier > 10% of base loss, LMC is broken.3.3 Representation: CKA (Centered Kernel Alignment)CKA measures the similarity of activations (hidden states), which is robust to rotation and permutation.Formula:where $K = X_A X_A^T$ and $L = X_B X_B^T$ are the Gram matrices of activations for a batch of data.JAX Implementation:Pythondef linear_cka(activations_a, activations_b):
    # Act:
    # Center columns
    a = activations_a - jnp.mean(activations_a, axis=0)
    b = activations_b - jnp.mean(activations_b, axis=0)

    # HSIC(X, Y) = ||Y^T X||_F^2 / (n-1)^2
    # But for CKA ratio, the scaling factors cancel out.
    numerator = jnp.linalg.norm(b.T @ a, ord='fro') ** 2
    denom_a = jnp.linalg.norm(a.T @ a, ord='fro')
    denom_b = jnp.linalg.norm(b.T @ b, ord='fro')

    return numerator / (denom_a * denom_b)

Mitigating Catastrophic ForgettingWhen merging Branch A (Diffusion) and B (Rules), the merged model tends to forget the "Rules" (Branch B) as it continues training on A's data, or vice versa.5.1 Experience Replay (Recommended)This is the most robust solution for RL.Mechanism: Each branch maintains a small buffer (5-10%) of the other branch's data.Branch A trains on: $90\% \mathcal{D}_{diff} + 10\% \mathcal{D}_{rule}$.Branch B trains on: $90\% \mathcal{D}_{rule} + 10\% \mathcal{D}_{diff}$.JAX: Use jax.random.choice to mix batches dynamically.5.2 Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC)Use the Fisher matrix computed for Fisher Merging.Add penalty to loss: $\lambda \sum_i F_i (\theta_i - \theta^*_{old})^2$.This protects the weights that were critical for the previous merged state.6. Implementation Deep Dives6.1 Fisher Information in JAX (15M Params)For a 15M parameter model, the diagonal Fisher vector consumes $\approx 60$ MB of memory.Efficiency: Use jax.vmap over the batch dimension of jax.grad.Batching: You don't need the Fisher on the entire dataset. A representative sample of 1024-4096 transitions is sufficient for a stable estimate.6.2 Is Git Re-Basin Worth It?Yes. For trading agents, the signal-to-noise ratio is low. SGD is driven heavily by noise, meaning the probability of symmetry breaking is near 100%. Without Re-Basin, you are likely averaging noise with signal. The computational cost (minutes every few hours) is negligible compared to the cost of training a failed model.6.3 Layer-wise StrategyEncoder: Merge with Fisher. We want a shared "Market View."Heads: Keep separate (Ensemble) or merge with TIES. Policy decisions are fragile.6.4 Optimizer State MergingCrucial: Do not reset the optimizer state (Adam momentum $m$, variance $v$).Merge Rule: $m_{new} = 0.5 m_A + 0.5 m_B$, $v_{new} = 0.5 v_A + 0.5 v_B$.Why?: Resetting $v$ to 0 effectively resets the learning rate adaptation, causing a massive spike in update magnitude (shock) that can eject the model from the basin.ConclusionTo achieve >80% oracle agreement, simple FedAvg is insufficient. The recommended pipeline is:Train with FedProx to tether trajectories.Monitor CKA and Barrier Height.Merge using Fisher Merging for the Encoder and TIES-Merging for the Heads.Align using Git Re-Basin before any hard merge.Preserve knowledge using Experience Replay.This holistic approach addresses the geometric, probabilistic, and architectural challenges of federated RL in financial domains.
