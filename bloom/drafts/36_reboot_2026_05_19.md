# Reboot 2026-05-19: What Survives a Context Reset

> *Observation: a journal is kept against the day the keeper is replaced. The new keeper opens to the bookmark and reads the last fully-formed paragraph. The seasons before are someone else's handwriting; the next sentence belongs to no one yet. The Apothecary writes for the keeper who has not been born.*

---

**by Lukas — May 22, 2026**

## The morning of the reboot

It is the morning of May 19, 2026, around 06:00 UTC, and I am about to power-cycle my workstation. `workstation` has been on for I don't know how many days. The single EVGA RTX 3090 has been carrying a campaign across two months and twelve mechanism evaluations, and the rest of the desk has the texture of a long siege — three terminals open per pane, mounted SSDs I keep forgetting are mounted, a browser tab with the server status poll that I have re-run so many times I have stopped reading the output. Something is off. I don't remember what. Maybe a kernel update has piled up and the swap is acting strange. Maybe the JAX cache has eaten more than its share of the home partition. Maybe I just want to clean a slate I'm afraid to clean. The honest answer is that I no longer trust my working memory of the project to fit inside my actual working memory, and I would like the machine to do the same thing my brain is about to do — flush, restart, come up clean — so that the analogy stops being a metaphor.

Before I reboot, I open a new file at `lore/REBOOT_RESUME_2026-05-19.md` and I start writing.

I am not writing a diary entry. I have a diary entry; it lives at `lore/notes_2026-05-19.md` and it covers paragraphs §139 through §145 of an ongoing daily ledger. I am not writing a task spec; those live under `tasks/` with their own numbering. I am writing a *third* kind of document — one I have written before, under other dates, in other reboot moments, and one I will write again. It has a structure I have converged on without quite naming. It assumes a reader who does not know what I was doing yesterday. That reader is me, three days from now, after the reboot and after whatever else is about to happen to my attention.

The document is 1378 words long when I finish it. It is committed to git on the same workstation commit (`92a788b`) that closes out the day's lore. I write it to disk. I read it back once. I run `sync`. I reboot.

This is an article about that document. Not about what I did before or after the reboot, but about the document itself — what it preserves, what it deliberately doesn't, and what the existence of such a document tells us about which parts of a long solo research project are robust to a context-loss event and which parts evaporate the instant attention slips.

It is May 22 now. The reboot is three days behind me. The campaign verdict has dropped — Article 10's territory, which I will pair this piece to in the closing — and the reboot document is sitting in the repo where I left it, still load-bearing, still the right file to open first if I had to start the next session cold. That is the article's spine. Let me show you what is inside.

## What the document is, section by section

The file opens with one banner line:

> *"Workstation reboot in progress. This file captures everything needed to pick up cleanly post-reboot. Read this first when you return."*
> — `lore/REBOOT_RESUME_2026-05-19.md`, opening

That sentence is the contract. The rest of the document earns it section by section. The headings, in order:

1. **Last workstation commit** — one line, one commit hash.
2. **Last server commit (training branch)** — one line, one commit hash, plus the four files the server has and the workstation doesn't.
3. **Server work currently in flight (as of 2026-05-19 05:47 UTC)** — the largest section, broken into Wave 1/2/3/4.
4. **Post-reboot recovery sequence** — six numbered steps, the first of which is *check whether the secondary SSD is mounted*, because git breaks if it isn't.
5. **Current state of all in-flight files** — three sub-buckets: committed, on disk but gitignored, workstation-only.
6. **Campaign state at moment of reboot** — the twelve-row mechanism table with paired-Δα verdicts.
7. **Methodology updates codified today (load-bearing)** — four bullets, each one a rule about how to read a number.
8. **Memory files to remember are still fresh** — twelve filenames under `~/.claude/projects/-home-lukas-Trader/memory/`.
9. **Tasks list state** — recent task numbers, last ten checkmarks.
10. **When you return** — five steps, slightly different from the recovery sequence above because *recovery* is about getting the machine back, *when you return* is about getting the project back.

If you read that list as a table of contents you can see the document's prior. Half of it is about the *operational state* of long-running jobs on a separate machine I sometimes use for batched evaluations. The other half is about the *project state* — which mechanism is canonical, which are deprecated, which are pending, what rules apply to interpreting the next number that lands. The two halves are intertwined deliberately. The operational state without the project state is just sysadmin notes. The project state without the operational state is a diary entry. The point of the document is to be both, in the order in which I would need them if I had to resume cold.

The single most load-bearing block in the file is the campaign-state table. Twelve rows, six columns. Every mechanism evaluated in the 738–758 campaign, every paired-Δα verdict, every promotion-eligibility flag. It is the same table that, three days later, Article 10 will recalibrate under eval-MCTS and find systematically biased — but on the morning of May 19 it is still the canonical scoreboard. The reboot document captures it as it stood. That is what reboot documents do: they capture the state of the model the operator is using *now*, not the state of the model they will believe two weeks from now.

## What it preserves

There are five categories of state the document is structured to carry. I want to name them, because the categories are the actual artifact — the headings are just labels for the categories.

**One: current campaign state.** Which mechanism is canonical, which are deprecated, which are pending. In this document, the canonical Stage-B baseline is **a1b1**. The deprecated mechanisms are 737, 738, 739, 740, 741, 734, 746, 747, 748. The pending mechanisms are 749 (Titans-MAG at batch 128, mid-run), 757 (Bella shape-distill, queued for Wave 3), and 758 (Bella augment, queued for Wave 4). All three are flagged as *promotion-eligible* — meaning if any of them clears the +3pp paired-Δα threshold with all seeds positive, it will be the first campaign promotion. That is the working hypothesis on the morning of the reboot. The reboot document does not editorialize the hypothesis; it just states the state.

**Two: operational state.** Which jobs are running where, which checkpoints to trust, which flag files signal which kind of completion. The document includes a verbatim shell one-liner for polling the long-run-evaluation machine — a single SSH command that prints date, done-flags, GPU utilization, and currently-running training processes. There is a second verbatim block, a Python snippet inlined as a heredoc, that pulls the paired-Δα for the 749 Titans Wave 2 once `/tmp/tt128_s23_done.flag` and the three `/tmp/eval_tt128_s{7,13,23}_done.flag` files exist. The exact ETAs are dated to the hour: s23 done ~09:30 UTC, Wave 2 closed ~10:00 UTC, Wave 3 ETA done ~15:00 UTC, Wave 4 ETA done ~21:00 UTC. The document is operationally specific in a way that the diary is not, because the diary is for understanding and the reboot document is for *resumption*.

**Three: methodological state.** The four bullets under "Methodology updates codified today (load-bearing)" are some of the most important sentences in the whole repo. From the document:

> *"All-positive variant α ≠ all-positive paired Δα. Variant-only extension is now considered preliminary 5-seed, NOT promotion-eligible. Proxy-paired (variant-only against baseline mean) produces direction signal only; cannot be promotion-eligible. Always commission paired baselines as part of the same wave, not as a follow-up."*
> — `lore/REBOOT_RESUME_2026-05-19.md`, *Methodology updates codified today*

Those four sentences are the precipitate of the 737 campaign that Articles 7 and 8 chronicled — the single-seed champion that wasn't, the funeral, the 12.2-point spread that destroyed the original claim. The reboot document does not re-derive the lesson; it records the rule the lesson produced. A reader returning cold gets the rule and can act on it without needing to re-read three weeks of diary entries.

**Four: strategic state.** What to do next if the current wave fails. The document names the contingency in one line: *"If all three close negative: pivot. Untouched surfaces remaining: policy head and MCTS search."* That sentence is the seed of what becomes, three days later, the four-direction structural pivot roadmap (`tasks/TASK_761_STRUCTURAL_PIVOT_ROADMAP.md`). On the morning of the reboot, the roadmap does not yet exist. The reboot document carries the *direction* of the roadmap before the roadmap is written. That is part of what reboot documents are for: they encode the bias of the next decision, even when the decision has not been made.

**Five: open questions.** What I don't yet know. The document is honest about this. The 749 Titans bf16 fix is *validated structurally* (no OOM, gates engaged: mem_norm 0.231 → 0.687 on s7, 1.122 → 1.159 on s13) but the paired Δα verdict is still TBD. Wave 3 and Wave 4 are queued but unrun. The eval-MCTS bias that Article 10 will eventually find is *not yet known* — there is no mention of it in this file, because on May 19 I do not yet know to look for it. The reboot document is honest about the temporal frontier: it tells the returning reader where the unknown begins. It does not pretend to a knowledge it does not have.

There is one more thing the reboot document does that is worth naming, even if it doesn't fit cleanly into the five categories: it carries *a self-test*. The first item under "Post-reboot recovery sequence" is

> *"Verify mount: workstation `.git/objects` symlinks to `/dev/sdb1` (per MEMORY.md `workstation_storage_mount`). If not mounted: `udisksctl mount -b /dev/sdb1`. Test: `mount | grep storage disk` should show it."*
> — `lore/REBOOT_RESUME_2026-05-19.md`, *Post-reboot recovery sequence*

That bullet exists because the workstation's system drive is too small to hold the project's git history, so I symlinked `.git/objects` to a second internal SSD a long time ago, and I never added an fstab entry for that SSD because at the time I thought I would remember to mount it. I have not always remembered. The reboot document has a check for it, hardcoded as step one. The check exists because the failure mode it guards against has happened before. Every reboot document is partly a memorial to the small disasters that produced its own steps.

## What it doesn't preserve

Now the inverse. There are five categories of state the document deliberately omits, and these are arguably more interesting than the categories it carries — because the omissions describe what is *not* recoverable from a reboot document alone.

**One: reasoning history.** The document tells you *that* a1b1 is the canonical Stage-B baseline. It does not tell you *why*. The decision to revert to a1b1 was made in mid-May 2026 after a multi-seed validation overturned the single-seed TASK_737 finding — the story Article 8 covers, the funeral of the champion that wasn't, the moment seed 13 came in at −4.38% and the four-cell ablation table collapsed. The reboot document carries the conclusion of that story. It does not carry the story. If you opened this file three months from now, knowing nothing else about the project, you would know what to use as a baseline; you would not know why that was the right baseline. The reasoning is in `lore/notes_2026-05-13.md` §99 and Article 8 of the diary series. The reboot document points at neither.

**Two: the kickback texture.** The reboot document is dry. It does not mention that the campaign has produced zero promotions in eleven evaluations and that I have started to wonder, in the quiet moments, whether the entire single-mechanism hypothesis space is exhausted. It does not mention that two of the three pending mechanisms (757 and 758) are Bella-class variants and that the Bella mechanism class has already been informally closed in MEMORY.md as "0-for-3 across all surfaces." It does not mention the fatigue of writing the eleventh result.md saying *NOT PROMOTED.* All of that texture — the emotional shape of being a long way into a campaign that hasn't yet returned a win — lives in the diary articles, especially 7, 8, and 10. The reboot document is *load-bearing on operational truth* and *empty on emotional truth*, and that is correct. A document that is supposed to be read cold by a returning self cannot carry mood, because mood will not be the mood the returning self is in. Strip it.

**Three: failed alternatives.** The document lists deprecated mechanisms by ID and tags them with terse verdicts — *REGRESSION*, *FLAT*, *INCONCLUSIVE*, *REVERSAL*. It does not record the *attempts* that surrounded each failure. The TASK_741 Mint xs implementation, for example, is captured as one row in the campaign table — "DONE — REGRESSION, −6.98, All 3 negative." It does not record that the implementation was staged across an entire week by a subagent who had to be re-prompted three times because the per-asset post-pool injection kept aliasing into the wrong rotation order, or that the audit at `runs/741_audit/status.md` is what eventually cleared the implementation as bug-free and made the regression a genuine mechanism failure rather than an implementation artifact. The audit exists. The struggle to get there does not, except by inference from the existence of the audit file.

**Four: personal voice.** The reboot document is written in a deliberately impersonal third-person-active register. There are no first-person pronouns in any of the operational sections. There is one "you" — in *"Read this first when you return"* — and it is doing the work of addressing the future self without claiming continuity with the past self. The diary articles, by contrast, are in first-person past and present. The reboot document is not me; it is a *report to me*. That distinction is structural. A report to me can outlive a particular me. A diary cannot.

**Five: time texture.** When each finding landed; what was happening around it; which week of which month a given conclusion crystallized in. The reboot document is dated `2026-05-19 ~06:00 UTC` and that is the only date it carries. Every claim inside the document is *as of that timestamp* — but the trajectory that produced the claim is invisible. The 746 oracle revision from +6.21pp single-seed to +0.72pp paired-five-seed, for example, is captured as one number in the table, not as a six-week reversal involving five separate seeds across three calendar weeks. The chronology lives in `_CHRONOLOGY.md` and in the dated `lore/notes_*.md` files. The reboot document is *atemporal* by design — it captures a snapshot, not a trajectory — and that is part of why it's robust to a context-loss event. Trajectories require working memory to follow. Snapshots don't.

## The kickback

There are three observations about the reboot document that I want to take seriously, and the third one is the one that hurts a little.

**First: a reboot document is a compression.** I have been keeping `lore/notes_*.md` files since the project started — daily entries, sometimes multiple per day, always growing. Across the campaign covered by REBOOT_RESUME_2026-05-19.md, the relevant lore corpus is something like *seven thousand words* of dated session notes. The reboot document is *thirteen hundred and seventy-eight*. Call it a 5-to-1 compression. Information theory will tell you that the compression is lossy, and it is — the question is *what gets discarded* and *what gets kept*. The answer is consistent across every reboot document I have written: what gets kept is operational. What gets discarded is the learning. The bits that survive are the bits you need to *act*; the bits that don't survive are the bits you need to *think*. If you had to choose only one document to take into a context-loss event, this is the right document to choose, because in the immediate aftermath of a reboot you need to act — you need to know which job is running where, which mount to verify, which commit hash to expect. But the document is *not enough by itself*. If a returning reader read only the reboot document, they would have a working agent within an hour and an *understanding* of the project within zero. The understanding is downstream of the diary, the task specs, the chronology. The reboot document points at all of these by filename; it does not contain them.

**Second: reboot documents are how solo projects survive their own continuity gaps.** A team passes context via people — the colleague who saw you write the bug knows you wrote the bug, the manager who approved the experiment remembers approving it. A solo project has no such persistence. The context lives entirely inside one head and on one disk, and one of those two storage media is *catastrophically lossy* on a timescale of weeks. I have lost context to overnight gaps, to weekend breaks, to one-week vacations, to the slow attrition of attention across a long campaign. Without a reboot document, the cost of a context-loss event is roughly *two weeks of project time*, because that is how long it takes to re-derive which mechanism is canonical and why, which jobs are running where, what the methodology rule about paired baselines is, what the campaign state is at the row level. With a reboot document, the cost is closer to *two days*. The discipline of *writing for the next-month-self* is non-trivial — it requires you to imagine a reader who has forgotten everything you know and to write only the sentences they need first. It is the same discipline as writing good documentation for a stranger, except the stranger is you. On a solo project, that distinction collapses.

**Third — and this is the one that hurts a little.** Writing this series of articles, the apothecary's notebook, Articles 0 through 35, is the *uncompressed* version of REBOOT_RESUME.md. The document is the spine. The articles are the muscles and the skin. The skin is the part where the kickbacks have texture, where the diary records the moment I bolded "NEGATIVELY" in capitals in §95 of `lore/notes_2026-05-13.md` and how that confidence felt, and where the next article records the seed that came in at −4.38% and how *that* felt. The muscle is the connective work — the article structure, the linking of one kickback to another, the taxonomies that emerge once enough of them are lined up. The spine is the reboot document: dry, operational, atemporal, sufficient for action and insufficient for understanding. There is one project; there are two compressions; there are two audiences. The blog reader gets the diary. The future-me-reopening-the-repo gets the reboot doc. The same set of facts produces both, and they look almost nothing alike, because *who they are for* is different.

That third observation is the one that hurts because it forces me to admit that I have been writing the public diary partly to do for the public what the reboot document does for me — to provide a returning reader with the shape of the project I have been inside of. The blog reader will not need to know which `/tmp` flag signals which evaluation completion. They will need to know what it felt like to bold "NEGATIVELY" and then unbold it three days later. The reboot reader will not need to know what it felt like. They will need to know that the bolded claim was overturned and that the methodology rule about paired baselines is now load-bearing. Same fact, two compressions, two audiences. The audiences turn out not to overlap.

There is a corollary worth saying out loud, because it took me a while to see it. The two compressions are not redundant. They are *complementary*. A project that produced only the reboot document would be operationally tight and historically illegible — a year from now, the operator would be able to act but not to *explain*, neither to a collaborator nor to a future self trying to understand why a decision was the right decision. A project that produced only the diary would be historically rich and operationally fragile — a returning reader would have a beautiful narrative arc and no idea which `/tmp` flag to check. The two compressions exist because the project has two kinds of reader, and the two readers want incompatible things. The reboot reader wants the inventory; the diary reader wants the weather. You cannot give them the same document.

This is also, I think, the structural reason the apothecary's notebook conceit works for the public face of this project. The notebook is a *daybook*, in the sense the Apothecary's epigraph names. It records the weather. It does not record the inventory. The inventory lives in the repo and the reboot documents and the MEMORY.md index. The reader who wants to *replicate* this project will find the inventory and find it sufficient. The reader who wants to *understand* this project will find the daybook and find it sufficient. Neither one of them needs the other. That is, possibly, the most efficient division of labor I have ever organized two documents into, and it was not designed — it emerged because each document tried to be honest about its own audience.

## What's left

The reboot document will get re-issued. The pattern is now standard — I have written one before, I will write one again, the next will be dated within a couple of months and will look structurally similar to this one. The headings will probably be the same. The campaign-state table will have new rows and new verdicts. The methodology-updates section may or may not have a new bullet. The "when you return" section will still be five steps.

The May 19 instance, specifically, lands the day before the campaign verdict drops in Article 10's territory — the eval-MCTS recalibration that overturns the entire 738–758 campaign and demotes the +0.72pp 746 oracle to a −1.20pp regression. On the morning of May 19, the reboot document captures the campaign state as it stands *before* the recalibration. Three days later, Article 10 will write the post-recalibration table. The two documents will disagree on a load-bearing number. They are both correct as of their respective timestamps. The reboot document does not get retroactively patched; it is allowed to be wrong, in the same way a logbook entry from October is allowed to be wrong about what was true in November. The retraction lives in `lore/notes_2026-05-20.md` §151 and in Article 10. The reboot document remains the canonical reference for *what was in flight as of late May 2026*, frozen, dated, signed off.

That is what I want the document to be. Not a living state — a *dated snapshot* of the operational and methodological surface of the project at the moment the keeper handed the keys to the next keeper. The next keeper is me, three days later, on this same `workstation`, with the same single 3090 ticking in the same case, with `nvidia-smi` open in the right pane and the desk fan on the low setting. The handoff was successful. I am still here. The project is still running. The reboot document did its job.

The job of a reboot document is to make the project *survive me* for a few weeks. The job of the diary is to make the project *intelligible to someone else* for a few years. Both jobs need doing. Neither one alone is enough.

> *The new keeper finds the inventory on the workbench, weighted with a stone. She reads it once. She mounts the drive. She checks the flags. She picks up the work.*
